# Sources and scope

Research checked 23 October 2026. Script paraphrases findings; the illustrations do not reproduce source figures or third-party app interfaces.

1. **Wood & Rünger (2016), Psychology of Habit.** https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417 — scenes 5–7: repeated responses in recurring contexts can become habitual. Review, not evidence that every checking session is automatic.
2. **Oulasvirta et al. (2012), Habits make smartphone use more pervasive.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2 — scene 6: short, repetitive checking can lead into other activities. Historical smartphone observations do not establish a universal effect size for current feeds.
3. **Lindström et al. (2021), A computational reward learning account of social media engagement.** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-19607-x — scene 13: social rewards can influence posting. This evidence concerns posting and likes; it is not direct proof about every act of scrolling. The script says this explicitly.
4. **Baughan et al. (CHI 2022), “I Don’t Even Remember What I Read”: How Design Influences Dissociation on Social Media.** https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3501899 — scenes 15–16: 43 US participants used a custom Twitter client across four weeks; interface interventions reduced self-reported normative absorption/dissociation. Not a clinical diagnosis, universal cure, or guarantee for all apps. Script uses “absorption” to avoid medicalizing the experience.
5. **Ruiz, Molina León & Heuer (MuC 2024), Design Frictions on Social Media: Balancing Reduced Mindless Scrolling and User Satisfaction.** https://doi.org/10.1145/3670653.3677495 ; open text https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18803v1 — scene 23: small experiment, 30 participants, reaction-before-next-post friction improved content recognition, with frustration reported by many participants. This does not test every suggestion in this video.

## Illustrative material and editorial judgments

Alex, the LOOP feed, bedtime clock change from 11:42 PM to 12:26 AM, bar lengths, and all depicted individual reactions are fictional. Bars do not represent measured wanting/enjoyment scores. Endless loading and autoplay are interface explanations, not numerical causal estimates. The idea that anticipation can outlast enjoyment is a description of the experience, not a brain chemistry measurement. No dopamine levels, addiction diagnoses, or brain-damage claims are made.

The timer, three saved clips, ten-minute boundary, charging shelf, alternative activity, and one-week trial are practical experiments, not a validated combined treatment. Availability of autoplay controls varies by app. A long session can be enjoyable and intentional; the script explicitly recognizes this. Goal: notice a decision point and make a choice, not maximize productivity.
